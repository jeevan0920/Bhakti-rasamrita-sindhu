## 2. The sight

### 2.1 The floor, and what it does to two screens

Eighteen points is the floor, and a point of this frame is not a typographic point: on a 390pt handset it is 0.183mm on the glass, and on a 320pt handset it is 0.181mm, because the smaller screen is also the smaller phone. That is why the floor is a floor and not a ratio. Eighteen of them is 3.29mm of em, and the x-height of Spectral at that size is 1.58mm.

Three consequences, and the third is the one that costs. The 320pt phone loses 70pt of column and keeps the whole 56pt band, so it loses 8 characters a line; it does not lose the band, because a margin that shrinks on a cheap phone was decoration on an expensive one — **on the screens that read.** H4 is right that the clock is not one of them, and the clock therefore gives the band and the gutter back: 56 and 16 out, 24 of left margin in, so the day's column is 342 on the 390 frame and **272** on the 320 rather than 224, and the clock reads at the width a clock should (A10). The act band does not follow the column. It stays at x 72 to 366 on every surface, because the hand learned one band and a hand is not taught twice (§3.1). The 568-tall phone holds nine lines of running text, which is 216 characters: the specimen's verse does not fit there and scrolls, at the same letter size, and at the reading size of 30/42 that screen sets six lines of verse — 114 characters — so a verse longer than that is read with the thumb on that phone and not otherwise. And on no screen does the letter get smaller to make a verse fit — there is no size below 18 and no leading below 24 anywhere in the product, and a build fails if either appears in the compiled style table.

### 2.2 What the eye is given, at three distances

The floor is not the size alone. It is size, ink and distance together, and this is the arithmetic I will defend. A person with a replaced lens and a scattering cornea keeps about 70% of the contrast the page gives them; that assumption is mine, it is stated, and if it is wrong the direction of every answer below is unchanged. The design must therefore supply 4.50 ÷ 0.70 = **6.43** where it means a sentence to be read.

That is also why the transliteration is `--ink-soft` (6.11) and not `--ink-faint`: it is the layer a person who cannot read Devanagari actually reads, and it is set at 18/26, the tightest leading in the product, where the gap between the line above's descender and this line's macron is two millimetres and no more. Twenty-six is the smallest leading at which those two clear each other. It is never reduced, and it is never clamped.

### 2.3 Large text: what reflows, what truncates, what the margin does

Nothing truncates. There is no ellipsis in this product, no `line-clamp`, no hidden overflow, no "read more". When the text grows, the column keeps its width, the type reflows downward, and the reading zone scrolls — the act zone never does. The only thing that leaves the screen is the position of the line, not the line. Two surfaces are the exception to the column's width, and both are argued below in this section: the verse, whose pane may be moved sideways when a word in it will not fit, because a word in it may not be broken; and the journal's field on S-07, whose block walks for the same reason and is the second surface and the last (I7). The page never scrolls; the block may.

**Three answers refused, and the one you sealed.**

The arithmetic above ends in a word that will not fit, and I would rather you had it from me than from the field. The three answers to it are refused, and they stay refused. A wider column at 300% is the margin given up, which is A5's direction reversed. A hyphens-at-300% exception breaks a word, which `01` §2.3 rule 7 forbids for a reason that survives: a hyphen promises a continuation, and in a verse the promise is false, because the word is whole. A 300% ceiling is the refusal this document exists to prevent. I wrote none of them, and I said I would not invent a fourth — and I did not have to. The three were answers that moved a word, the type or the page. The answer you sealed on Day 50 moves a fourth thing that none of them moved, and it moves it only where a word will not fit: the column. It is A14 here, D22 in `01` §12 and I6 in INTERLOCKS, and it is a consequence and not a mechanism: where every word fits there is no pane, no rule and no drag, and the product has done nothing.

**Two surfaces, and no third.**

The verse is a person's words, and a person is not broken to fit a column. When a word will not fit the column at the size the person has chosen, the column moves, and only the column. The page itself never scrolls horizontally, at any size and on any frame; the grid rule stands. The drag lives inside the block, between its own two edges, and it ends where the word ends. The journal's field on S-07 is the second surface and the last: at 300% its 294-point column is three points too narrow for a word of eleven characters (297), so its block is 386 at x 76 in a zone of 238 at 76–314, and the eye walks the 148 it cannot see. The words in the field are more a person's than the verse's, not less, so the principle reaches the field and does not stop at it (I7). The audit's field on S-08 is not a third: 351 at 54 is nine points past its 342-point column, one character, and it wraps where the hand is typing rather than walk under it — that is its page's exception, written at the end of this section and in I6.

**A fourth answer, where the pane cannot reach.**

The pane serves the two surfaces that hold a person's words. It cannot reach three others, and each of them does something the page can measure. S-08's audit field wraps his word at the character with no hyphen, because a field that moved under the hand that is typing is worse than the character the platform draws, and that is that page's exception and not a third licence (I6). S-09's foot line takes the largest step on the ladder at which its longest word fits — 54 at the 390 and 36 at the 320 — because a citation may not be broken. And S-11's *Janmāṣṭamī* takes the cap: 342 at 60 goes into neither 294 nor 224, a festival's name is not ours to rename, and the screen forbids a broken word, so the name stops at 36 and is 205 in the 224. Because the pane cannot reach this surface, a fourth answer is now needed, and it is named here: **a word of ours stops where the place can hold it whole, and the number is written beside it.** The door's word already stops at 36 and the act's verb at 40/56 (S-08, R-08 · 3); the name is the third and the one that forces the naming. It is not the 300% ceiling this page refuses, because that was a cap on him: a word of his is never set smaller than the size he asked for, and a build that does it fails. It is the third answer narrowed until it is no longer the ceiling it was, and it is A15.

**The pane and the column are two things, and the difference is the whole of it.** The pane is the window the person sees. The column is the measure the line is set in. At 300% the pane is the grid's column less one margin, and the column the line is set in is the width of the widest line, which here is one word: 414 for the nine-letter English word of the paragraph before. The 176 between the pane and the column is what the eye walks. A verse whose longest word fits at 300% keeps the full 294 and shows no rule, because a band that opens without a walk is a promise of more where there is none.

**The margin at 300%.** The band holds at 56 and does not collapse, and it is the pane that gives: 294 − 56 = 238 on the 390 frame and 224 − 56 = 168 on the 320. The 56 is the margin's own width, and the verse gives it up rather than take the ācārya's band, so that the rule which says there is more stands where a margin stands and not against the glass. The frame then reads 56 + 16 + 238 + 56 + 24 = 390, and 56 + 16 + 168 + 56 + 24 = 320: two bands of one width with the verse between them.

**The walk, and what it costs.** 414 into 238 is 176 points of walk at 390; 414 into the full 294 would have been 120, so the pane's 56 is paid twice, once in the measure and once in the walk. It is paid because the other side of the trade is the margin, and the margin is the band that tells a person his verse has an address. At 320 the pane is 168 and the walk is 246, which is 70 more than at 390 and exactly the 70 points the smaller frame loses; the smaller the room, the more the eye walks, and the arithmetic says it before the sentence does. The field's walk is the same arithmetic and a different pair of numbers: its block is 386 and its zone 238, so the eye walks 148.

**What overflows, and why that is the proof.** English renderings almost never overflow at 300%. A nine-letter word is four point six em: 276 at 200% against a column of 294, which is 18 points of air and no pane; 359 at 78 where the verse stands, which is 121 beyond the 238 pane; and 414 at 90 on the reader, which is 120 beyond the full 294 and 176 beyond the pane. So the pane opens at 64 points, because 294 ÷ 4.6 = 63.9, and any rendering above 64 makes a nine-letter word wider than the whole column — which puts the opening between the table's 200% state and its 300% one. Sanskrit is where the corpus's own text overflows, and it overflows at the pane rather than at the column. कृष्णानुशीलनं is six akṣaras, 311.4 at 72 — 73.4 wider than the 238 pane, and 143.4 wider than the 168 — so it is a word that must be carried whole, and in the pane it takes a line of its own. And kṛṣṇānuśīlanam is fourteen characters at the half-em of `01` §2.3 rule 1, 378 at 54, which is 140 beyond the pane at 390 and 210 beyond it at 320. Those are precisely the strings that must not be hyphenated, because a compound broken at the column is a word made false — and that is why the pane is the only answer available to a word of his: the words that overflow are the words that may not break. None of them sets the block's drag, though. The drag is the width of the widest line in the block less the pane, and the widest line here is the rendering's nine-letter word at 414; the Sanskrit and the transliteration are carried inside it.

The pane opens for a word and not for a line. A pāda that breaks where the pane breaks is a line breaking, and a line breaks between two words; a compound that breaks inside itself is a word losing its joint. That difference is why the block moves and the type never does.

**The mark of more.** When there is more to the right — in the verse's block or in the field's — a 1pt rule stands at the block's right edge — `--ink-faint` by day and `--moon-faint` at night, the day's address ink and the night's rule ink, neither of them a letter (A8, I3). It is a rule and never a letter, which is I5's family: copper is a rule and a mark and never a letter, and this rule is that law in another ink. It is static. It is never animated, never a chevron, never an arrow, never a fade at the edge, and never a word; it is gone when the block is at its end and back when it is not, in one frame, with nothing moving. It is the only scroll affordance in the product — the only place where anything tells a person that there is more in a direction he cannot see.

**What does not change.** The act zone is at the foot in all three states and does not move with the pane; the door is 88 × 88 at x 72 to 160 with its word horizontal at 18; the provenance sentence stands at the foot in `--ink-soft` 8.73 in one of its two forms; and the reading order is the order it was, because the verse's block is announced as one unit — locator, Sanskrit, transliteration, rendering — and the field's block as one unit of his words, and neither drag reorders a word of it (§4.2). And no line is ever made smaller to make a verse fit: not by a scale, not by a maximum line-height, not by a fit-to-measure, and not in the pane. Nothing of a person's is shrunk. Nothing is hyphenated. Only the column is allowed to move — and, where a word of ours cannot go into its place at all, it stops at the size that fits, as A15 says.

**The sentence, for the draftsman.** *A compound is not broken to fit a column; the column is widened and the eye walks.* It is A14 in this document's table, D22 in `01` §12, and I6 in INTERLOCKS.

Two rules hold the large-text table together. **The reading zone scrolls and the act zone never does**, so the act is in the same place in all three states, and a hand that has learned it once does not have to learn it again. **Nothing is capped in its setting**: the leading is the type size multiplied by the same ratio in every state, because a maximum line-height is a refusal of the person's request disguised as tidiness. Four rows are not settings and are capped for reasons the rows give — the ring, which is a drawing; the door's word, which is bounded by the thumb's own strip; the act's verb, which stops at 40/56 because at 60 a six-letter verb is 180 in the act's 158 and would set in two lines; and a name the app prints, which stops at 36 because 342 at 60 does not go into 294 or 224 (the fourth answer above). Two rows are neither a setting nor a cap: the verse pane and the journal's field, which narrow and do not shrink. Those are the only exceptions, named so that a draftsman cannot invent another.

### 2.4 Devanagari at large sizes

At 24pt the śirorekhā is 1.9pt thick and an akṣara advances 17.3pt. At 300% they are 5.7pt and 51.9pt, and the arithmetic that matters is the width of a word.

| | 24pt | 48pt | 72pt |
|---|---|---|---|
| The śirorekhā | 1.9 | 3.8 | 5.7 |
| One akṣara's advance | 17.3 | 34.6 | 51.9 |
| `आनुकूल्येन`, 5 akṣaras | 86.5 | 173 | 259.5 |
| `कृष्णानुशीलनं`, 6 akṣaras | 103.8 | 207.6 | **311.4** |
| The column at 100% and 200%, the pane at 300% | 294 | 294 | **238** |

At 300% the second word is 311.4 in a measure of 238 — 73.4 wider than the pane, and 17.4 wider even than the 294 column would have been. It cannot be letterspaced (the śirorekhā would break between akṣaras, and the conjuncts would come apart), it cannot be faux-bolded, and it cannot be shrunk. And it is not broken. The akṣara break this paragraph carried until this return is withdrawn for the verse, because कृष्णानुशीलनं broken at an akṣara is a compound made false in the same way a hyphen makes it false: a space is where a line may break, and the joint inside a compound is not. The pane carries the word whole and the block's drag of 176 (§2.3) reaches past the 73.4 it needs. The build fails if any akṣara cluster's bounding box exceeds the pane at any of the three sizes, and it fails if a Devanagari word is broken at an akṣara where a pane exists. The akṣara break stands only for a Devanagari string set outside a verse block — a caption, a locator, a name — where there is no pane and the string must go into a band; the corpus prints no such string at 300% today, and that is said here so that the rule is not mistaken for one with a place to live.

The pāda is a line at 100% and a paragraph at 300%. An anuṣṭubh pāda of eight syllables is about twelve akṣaras, which at 72pt is 622pt against the 238pt pane; the pāda therefore breaks where the pane breaks, and the breath gap of 36pt still marks where the pāda ended. Devanagari is never rotated, never justified, never tracked, and never given a maximum line-height: at 300% the deepest stack in the corpus descends 30.2pt below its baseline, and the 120pt leading clears it by 45.

### 2.5 Nothing is written on a photograph

There are **two** photographs in the product, and no text is ever placed over either — not the locator, not the seal, not a caption, not the act's name: the **form** on S-04, and the **lamp** on the ārati. Round 1 said there was one, and that sentence is struck; it was the sentence that removed the vibhāva from the room named for seeing it (objection 1, and §4.5 below).

There are four reasons for the rule, and the first three would hold if the product had twenty photographs. Every value in `01-FOUNDATIONS.md` is a ratio computed from two hex values, and over a photograph there is no second hex value, so there is no ratio and the guarantee is gone at the first pixel. `--lamp` measures 3.15 on paper and 4.46 on kunda, and a caption over a flame would be a letter on a flame. The photograph is a subject: a caption over a subject is a second subject, when this product has one subject to a screen. And a caption is a sentence, which means it would be the only sentence on a screen with no ink to be measured in.

The fourth is the one that makes the rule a rule. Each photograph is the **column's width**, and each has the shape of the thing it is a photograph of: the form is **294 × 441** — 2:3, because a form is not a square — at x 72 to 366, y 84 to 525 on the reference frame; the lamp is **294 × 294** at y 113 to 407, because a flame is. On the 320 frame both are the column's 224 wide and their heights follow, 336 and 224, because a 294 image would run 70 points off the glass. That is not a second size, it is the same size taken from the column: `02` §8's *one size and one only* is this rule drawn on the 390 frame. An image that does not fit the cheap phone is an image that only the expensive phone has, and this document is written from the cheap phone. Neither image comes within 24pt of an edge, and neither is ever scaled to fill a screen or to follow the type — an image does not grow when a person asks for larger text, which is why A9's 62% and the column's width are the only two numbers a photograph has.

Each provenance is a text fact and lives in the margin, and the two are written the same way and are not the same fact. The lamp's is the one already written: Rādhā-kuṇḍa, the hour before sunrise, the fourteenth of March, one exposure, nothing removed. The form's adds what a form needs and a flame does not: that the object is stone, that the frame is 2:3 because the object is taller than it is wide, and that nothing was removed — and it carries the same place and the same morning, because the two were photographed in the same hour at the same kuṇḍa, and a provenance that invented a second morning to look interesting would be the first retouching in the corpus. The offering's word lives in the band, below the image, where the hand can reach it.

**The form is not ornament.** H9 forbids the sacred used as a texture, and a lotus set behind a verse is a curtain; a photograph of the form, with its provenance in the margin, is the object itself, and your charter puts the form first among the vibhāvas (§4). The distinction is not a defence; it is the reason the photograph has a margin and a date instead of a border and a caption.

**And the hardest test in this document.** A photograph of a real form has cloth, flowers and paint in it, and this palette has no red. The image may not be retouched beyond crop and exposure, which is your rule (§7), so the only levers are the hour, the angle and the crop — and they are used *before* the frame is accepted, not after. The 12% red-band test of `01` §1.3 is applied to both photographs (test 6, §10). If no honest frame of the form passes it, S-04 is not built and the frame is brought to you with the reason; a red thing on a screen with no red is the one fault this palette cannot absorb, and a form shown in the wrong colour is worse than a room left empty for a week.

### 3.2 The 48pt floor, and the one place it is not enough

The minimum target is 48 × 48 — 2,304 square points. The band as a whole is 294 × 88, which is 25,872, or 11.2 of them; inside it the act is 158 × 88 = 13,904, **6.0 of them**, and the exit strip is 88 × 88 = 7,744, **3.4**. The act lost 40 points of width in Round 2 when the door widened (objection 7) and is still six targets, still 158 points across, and still wider than any thumb. The bell is exactly 2,304, and the note's mark on S-07 is exactly 2,304; they are the two targets in the product that require a thumb to aim, and the difference between them is the whole of this section. **The bell is never essential**: it arms a sound that the ārati does not need in order to be the ārati. **The note's mark is essential**, because for the man who cannot type the note is the only form the act has (S-07 §2, §5 [E], §11). The 48 at x 318–366 is the bell's place on the ārati and the note's mark on S-07; the exception is the place and not the sound. The price is the screen's own and is not hidden here: 76 to 103 from the right pivot, 275 to 327 from the left, comfort for a right thumb and past the 300 stretch at a left thumb's far corner — a man who works with his left hand finds the far part of the mark past the stretch, and S-07 §11 prints that cost rather than absorbing it. The third place holds the bell or the note's mark and never both, because the bell is the ārati's and S-07 has no ārati. Nothing else is a control. Text is not tappable. The margin is not tappable. A word is not a link. There is no slider, no switch, no stepper, no pull-to-refresh, no tab bar, no drawer, no carousel. The whole interactive surface of the product is one band, one strip, and in the third place either the bell on the ārati or the note's mark on S-07.

### 3.3 What the hand is never asked to do

| Refused for anything essential | Why it fails a real hand |
|---|---|
| A two-finger gesture of any kind | Rāmāi has one; Yaśodā has one because the other holds a child; a pinch, a rotate and a two-finger scroll all need two |
| A long press | it is invisible, it requires stillness that a tremor and a grief do not have, and its duration is a number nobody knows |
| A swipe | it has no affordance, it collides with the platform's own edge gestures, and it leaves no mark that it was done |
| A double tap | it is the reader's activation gesture, so it cannot also be a shortcut; and it is fast, which a shaking hand is not |

| A timed press, or anything that expires while the finger is down | the act must wait for as long as the hand takes |
| An act at the top of the screen | see 3.1; the numbers are not close |
| An act in the margin | 508pt from the right pivot, 401 from the left |

Two allowances go the other way, and both are in favour of the hand. The band accepts a press that slides off it and still counts, because a thumb rolls. And the band ignores a second activation within 400ms, because a tremor rebounds and a person who means one bead must not be given two. **On a sheet the first allowance splits, and the split is the sheet's one departure from this page.** A press that slides off *Keep* counts for *Keep*. A press that slides off the verb that ends a page counts for nothing: the sheet stays up, the words stay in the field, and the note he has just made stays with them — a slide-off never deletes a page, and it can never lose a note that was being taken. A thumb that rolls may end a sentence but may not end a page, and a note is a sentence in the man's own voice (S-07 §5 [F], §6, §7, §10.9). The loss needs a second, deliberate press, and the sheet's own words say what will be lost before either press is made.

The table above is about acts, and there are two movements in the product that are not acts. The verse pane and the journal's field of §2.3 are scrolls: the same gesture as the reading zone's vertical scroll, on a block instead of on a screen, with no target and no press state. A drag is refused here because a drag on a control asks a hand to hold one end and reach the other; a pane asks nothing of the hand that already scrolls a page. A person who cannot use the gesture at all is not left with a broken word: the platform's own scroll actions move the block for a switch, a keyboard or a reader, and the block is announced whole whether it has moved or not (§4.2).

## 4. The screen reader

There are two laws, and everything in this section is those two sentences worked out. **The first law: the ring is understandable without sight** — a person who has never seen it must be able to say, from the announcement alone, what the app expects and when. **The second law: a count of the person is never announced by default** — not a bead, not a day, not a junction. What the reader hears is not the visual order; it is the order in which a person would be told the same thing if he were sitting beside it.

| # | The stop | What is announced | What is never announced at this stop |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The screen | its name, and the one sentence that says what it is for | its position in a stack, a count of screens, a back button's name |
| 2 | The ring, on the day only | what the day expects, and when; the shape; the fact that no number is in it | how many junctions are kept, how many missed, a percentage, an ordinate |
| 3 | The verse | Sanskrit, transliteration, rendering — in that order, each named before it is read | a summary of the verse, a mood, a tag, "verse of the day" |
| 4 | The colophon | the locator, then the mark | the rotated glyph order, the margin as a place |
| 5 | The act | its name, and the one line at the foot of the reading surface — which on a reading screen is the chain, and on every other screen is what will happen and how long it takes | a second act, a suggestion, a "you may also like" |
| 6 | The way out | where it goes | "back", a stack, a count of what is behind it |

The fifth stop changed in Round 2 and the change is the whole of objection 2. On the reader the act is now the hearing — *Hear it* — because the reader's aṅga is 43 and not 4 (H5), and the line at the foot of the reading surface is no longer the encounter with the librarian but the chain: the work, the verse, the reviewer, the date and the translator, in that order, spoken once. The full chain is at S-09 Ācārya, which is a room a person walks to and not a thing pulled out of a verse. A person who came to hear is not handed a catalogue; he is handed the hearing, and one line that tells him who stood behind the words.

### 4.1 The ring

> "The day. Eight acts, from maṅgala at 04:20 to niśā at midnight. The next act is maṅgala-ārati, at 04:20. The arcs that have closed are filled; the arcs that were missed are left open and are not marked. No number is spoken here."

That is one stop, not eight. The ring is a drawing of a day and not a list of eight items, so it is announced as a shape, and the only fact taken from it is the next act. A second announcement follows if the person stays, and H7 rewrote it:

> "The ring. Eight ticks, and the arc between each pair is one act of the day. The arcs are equal, because the acts are counted and the hours are not: a long arc does not mean a long practice and a short one does not mean a short one, and the hour is marked by a single line. The ring does not count you, and it does not count the time down."

The second sentence is the one that was added. In Round 1 the arcs were minutes — 0.5323 of a point a minute — and an arc's length therefore weighed a man's act by the length of the hour it sat in; the fill now means eight acts and not the passage of the day, and the announcement says so, because a spoken ring that left the old meaning in it would be a drawing with a lie in its mouth.

**What is never announced**: how many arcs are filled, how many are empty, the day's fraction, a "streak", a "best", the number of days since the last act, or any sentence that begins with *you have*. There is no gesture that produces those numbers, because there is no number to produce. If a person asks the app *how am I doing*, the app has nothing to say, and that silence is the answer the book gives: a tally in the margin is a debt collected daily. The first law is tested here and not in the code: after one hearing, a person must be able to say what the app expects at 04:20, and what the ring is not.

### 4.2 The verse block

| Part | The label string | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| The Sanskrit | "Sanskrit, two lines, in Devanagari. If your phone has a voice for Devanagari, it will read them. If it does not, the transliteration follows." | the two lines, or the fallback sentence, and then the transliteration |
| The transliteration | "Transliteration, four lines." | the four lines, read as words and not as letters |
| The rendering | "Rendering. Not a translation. The translator is named in the chain, or the chain says that no translator is named." | nine lines at 30/42, with a 200ms pause at each breath break |
| The locator | "Locator. Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu, division one, chapter one, verse eleven. The line at the foot names the reviewer and the day, or says that no reviewer is named. The full chain is at the provenance sheet." | — |
| The mark | "The mark is present. A named reviewer has checked this rendering." or "The mark is absent. No reviewer is named for this rendering. The line at the foot says so, and the verse is not withheld for it." | — |

Two rules are inside those strings. The Sanskrit is never announced as a row of letters: a Devanagari voice reads it, or the app says plainly that the phone cannot, and offers the transliteration as the thing it does have. And the rendering is never announced as *the* translation, because it is not one; the word *rendering* is the app's own honesty about its own words, and it is spoken every time.

The pane of §2.3 and the field's block add no string. The verse's block is one unit whether the pane is open or closed: the reader hears the locator, the Sanskrit in its language, the transliteration and the rendering, once each, in that order, and moving the pane never reorders a word of it, because the pane moves the window and not the text. The journal's field is one unit in the same way: his words are read once, in the order he wrote them, and the walk to the 148 the window cannot show does not move a word of them (I7). If the platform announces a scrollable region, that is the platform's sentence and not this corpus's: no string here says *more*, and none may be added (I6).

### 4.3 The bead

The bead is the one act with no controls, no image, and no count spoken. How it is described is the whole test of whether this app can be understood without sight.

> "The name. The screen is dark. Every press on the glass is one bead. Nothing is counted aloud. The count is not spoken unless you ask for it, and then it is said once. The word at the left returns you to the day."

The count is spoken only when it is asked for, and it is one sentence: "Nine hundred and twelve beads." The close contains no number at all: "The session is closed."

That is the entire spoken design of the act. It satisfies the second law because the count is not spoken on entry, not spoken per bead, and not spoken at the close; it is spoken only when a person has asked, which is a deliberate act and never a default. The asking is the reader's own custom action on the bead — a one-finger swipe down while it is focused — and with the reader off it is a hold on the glass; neither is a long press on anything essential, and neither is the only way to end the session. It satisfies the first law because a person who has never seen the screen knows, from the first four sentences, where to press, what a press does, that his count is private, and how to leave. The screen's darkness is announced as a fact rather than hidden, because a person who cannot see must not be told that a black screen is a picture.

### 4.4 The margin

The margin is not a stop, and it is never a place the reader lands. A 56pt band of rotated type is not a region to walk through; it is a colophon, and it is spoken once, at the end of the verse block, in the fourth stop above. It is announced as a sentence — "Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu, division one, chapter one, verse eleven" — and never as a run of glyphs, because a rotated locator read letter by letter is a spelling test and not an address. It carries no control, so there is nothing to activate; the chain it points at is the line at the foot of the reading surface, and the full chain is at S-09 (objection 2). The margin tells a sighted person that a chain exists; the line tells everyone what is in it; and neither is a press.

### 4.5 The two photographs

The form, on S-04:

> "A form of the Deity, photographed at Rādhā-kuṇḍa in the hour before sunrise, on the fourteenth of March. The stone is stone and the photograph is an exposure of it; nothing has been added or coloured. The offering is at the foot of the screen."

The lamp, on the ārati:

> "A lamp, photographed at Rādhā-kuṇḍa in the hour before sunrise, on the fourteenth of March. The flame is one photograph and it does not move. The offering is at the foot of the screen."

Neither photograph is decorative and neither is announced as decoration. Each is a subject with a provenance, and the provenance is spoken in the same breath as the image, because the image's honesty is part of what it is — and because a person who cannot see must be told the difference between a form and a flame, which is the difference between the room named for seeing and the hour of the offering. The two were taken in the same hour of the same morning at the same kuṇḍa, so the place and the date repeat, and the repetition is a fact and not a template: the difference the ear needs is in the first clause, not in the address. The 2400ms brightening belongs to the lamp alone and is never announced or described as an animation: by the time the words are finished, the photograph is at full, and a person who cannot see has lost nothing that a person who can see has gained. The form does not rise. It is there in the frame the screen is painted, at every state, because a stone does not come up and a lamp does.

### 4.6 The room thread

> "The room. The Tuesday reading. One host and eleven others, and the room holds no more than thirty. The thread is read oldest first. The act is to bow."

The thread is read in the order it was spoken, with the speaker's name before the sentence, and no number is attached to any reply. The bow, when it is offered:

> "The bow was offered. Nothing was counted."

And when one is received:

> "A bow was offered to you. This week, eleven."

The asymmetry is deliberate and it is audible: the sender is told that nothing was counted, and the recipient is given a total. It is the only place in the product where a number about people is spoken, and it is a number about a room of thirty, which cannot grow.

**The death in a room.** The bereaved state is a room whose host has died, and the one sentence the ear must hear is the one the screen prints in the head where the host's name stood: *Kamalā has died.* It is spoken at the stop where the host's name was spoken, once, and the stop does not change — a person who has learned nine stops does not learn a tenth at the hour of a death. The shape is the death, then the room's closure, then the thread, then the door:

> "Kamalā has died. The room is closed." Then the thread, in the order it was spoken, each word with its speaker's name before it. Then the door: "close."

The first two are the screen's own strings (S-06 §8). The third is §4.6's order and not a new sentence about the death: the names and the words that were in the room stay, and nothing is added to them after the death. The fourth is the door, and it is the last thing reachable. What the ear is never given at this stop, in this state or any other: the room's hour, the window word, a bell, a rite, a condolence, a question, a count of the days since, or an announcement of the app's own silence. The living room's stop says *Kept by Kamalā Devī. The room sits on Tuesday at half past six.*; the bereaved stop says the death and the closure and nothing else, because the room does not sit, and a sentence that says it does is the one promise the state was rebuilt to withdraw (R-06 · Round 2, §3).

The register is the register of a fact. The verb is *has died*, the name is before it, and it is said once. Not *passed away*, not *lost*, not *gone*, not *at peace*, not *in a better place*; not *our condolences*, not *in loving memory*, not *we*, and no sentence that reports a sorrow the app does not have. The death is entered by a person and never inferred from a phone that stops opening; the app does not reopen the room, does not keep its hour, and does not mark the day. This is `03` §7.4's line and room with its act struck, because the sixty-four give no act of mourning and S-06's band holds the door alone (§3, §5 [A], [B], [D], §6, §11).

### 4.7 The terminal dim

> "The session has ended. The door opens the quiet page, and nothing is waiting."

One announcement, once, and then the reader is released. It names the door and what it opens, because the door opens a page and not a closing, and a person who cannot see must not be told that the app is shut when a page is about to be painted. **What is behind the door is the quiet page** — the state the app is in when it is closed — and it may be there for three reasons and no fourth: it asks nothing; one door leaves it, and that door is *the day*; and it is the address of the three things the close must say and no other surface can hold — the bells' arming rule and its window, the ledger's one line that no number is kept, and at most one offer, offered once, declined by the door, and never asked again (S-10 §2, §5 [A], §8, §10). It is not a second product and not a settings list, which is what the older sentence was written to prevent: it has no switch, no account, no list, and no way back into the session that has ended.

*Nothing is waiting* remains true, and it is the sentence that carries the announcement: the page holds no question, no act that is required, and no word about the session. The announcement does not repeat, does not announce the dimming as an event, does not say *one line remains* — the page it opens holds eight lines and a name, and a person told of one line and given eight has been told a smaller truth than the page — and does not offer a way back except the quiet page's door, which is the day. A person who cannot see must be told that the screen has gone quiet, or he will sit in front of a black rectangle wondering whether it has failed.

The reader's own furniture is four rules and no ornament. The focus ring is a 2pt `--tulasi` ring with a 2pt `--paper` gap and a 2pt `--ink` caret to the left of the focused line, so focus is never shown by colour alone. The reading order is fixed and does not change with the state of the screen: a person learns six swipes and keeps them for the life of the app. The act is reachable in **five swipes** from the day and **eight** from a verse, and the pass line in §10 is twelve. And no announcement is longer than the verse — the ring, the act and the exit are each under forty words, because a sentence that takes longer than the act is a sentence that has become the act.

### 5.2 The crack, and the four per cent

A crack is not a metaphor. Sundara's runs across the digitiser from x=0 to x=390 at y 690 to 724, and it does not register a touch. The day still opens, the act still completes, the name still counts, and the reader still reads. An act that needs 12% of a battery is an act for a person with a charger.

## 6. The grief state

There is no grief mode. A mode would require the app to know, and it must not know: your R10 forbids inferring an inner state, and a menu item called *I am bereaved* is a form to fill in at the hour of loss. Everything below is therefore true at 03:40 on any day, for anyone, with no setting and no choice.

| The app removes, always | The app keeps, always | The app must never show, at any hour |
|---|---|---|
| a countdown to the next junction | the hour's act, if there is one | a verse about death chosen by the app |
| the number of junctions kept or missed | the name, at every hour | "he is in a better place", or any consolation the app is not qualified to give |
| the room's thread from the day | the verse, if the person opens it | a memory, an anniversary, a "one year ago" |
| a prompt to write, to breathe, to reflect | the way out, at the same place | a question in the second person: *how are you*, *would you like to* |
| the welcome back, the summary, the greeting | the dim, and the silence after it | a photograph of a person, a candle, a dove, a heart |
| the bell, unless armed for this ārati | the reader, at the same order | a suggestion to talk to someone, in a cheerful register |
| a verse of the day | the bell, if he armed it | a count of days since, or a "day 11" of anything |

The refusals are gate work, not taste. A verse chosen by the app for a bereaved person is the app interpreting a grief it cannot see, and `BRS 4.9` calls the counterfeit of a rasa worse than no rasa: aestheticised grief is an `aparasa`, and it trains the palate to accept the false at the one hour when the person has no defence. `BRS 1.2.80` puts the same thing in the second ten of the aṅgas: one is not to be carried away by gain and loss, and one is not to be carried away by lamentation either. The app's part in that is to stop talking.

### 6.1 The one act, in three taps from anywhere

The act is the name, because the name is the one aṅga the book gives no hour. `BRS 1.2.87` gives ārati its different times; `BRS 1.2.149` and `1.2.150` give japa its softness and its loudness and no hour at all. So the hours with no act — nirmalya at 05:45, niśā at midnight — are not empty: they offer the name.

| From | Tap 1 | Tap 2 | Tap 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| The threshold | the exit strip: "the day" | — | — |
| Any reading screen | the exit strip: "close" | the day | the name |
| A sheet | the sheet's close | the day | the name |
| The day | the exit strip: "the name" | — | — |
| The name | the exit strip: "the day" | — | — |

The promise is three taps. The design gives one from the threshold and two from the day and two from any reading screen, with the third held in reserve for the case where a sheet is open — and it is measured with a stopwatch in §10, not counted on a finger. The threshold's room is one press because it has opened nothing to close: the first frame's door is *the day*, and *close* stays on the surfaces where something has been opened (S-01 §5 [E], R-01 · 3).

The floor plan, in one line, is the whole navigation of the product: **the threshold goes to the day; any screen closes to the day; the day's way out is the name; the name's way out is the day.** There is no stack to get lost in, no drawer, no tab, and no place where a person can be three levels deep at 03:40 with no way to the one act that asks nothing.

What makes it usable in grief is not the path but what is absent from it. Between Śānti's screen and the bead there is no question, no permission, no confirmation, no sound, no count, no image, and no sentence that asks her to be calm. The band takes a press from a shaking hand, ignores the rebound, and opens the dark.

## 7. Motion and vestibular safety

Five tokens, and none of them moves anything through space except the sheet's 24pt rise. That is the whole of the vestibular surface of the product.

| Token | What it does | With reduced motion | What the person still perceives |
|---|---|---|---|
| `--settle` | a sheet rising 24pt; one block dissolving | 0ms, a cut, no rise | that the screen changed |
| `--quick` | the press state of the band | 0ms, a colour swap | that the press was taken |
| `--fill` | a segment closing; a mark appearing | 0ms, the shape is simply closed | that the act was accepted |
| `--lamp-rise` | the photograph cross-dissolving over 2400ms | **the photograph is present at full from the first frame** | the lamp |
| `--dim` | the ground receding over 1200ms | 0ms, one frame | that the session ended |

`--lamp-rise` is the one the seal asks about, and the rule is exact: **at reduced motion it becomes a static state.** Not a faster fade, not a shorter one, not a cross-dissolve at 0% to 100% over an imperceptible duration — the photograph is there, at full, in the frame the screen is painted. The 2400ms exists because a hand raising a lamp takes two and a half seconds; a person who has asked the phone to stop moving things has not asked for the lamp to be raised faster, only for it not to rise.

The rules that keep the rest safe, stated as the negative list they are:

- Nothing oscillates. Not one thing in the product moves back and forth, at any frequency, for any reason. A flame that flickers would be an oscillation; the flame is one photograph and there is no second frame.
- Nothing zooms, scales, rotates, or travels more than 24pt, and the 24pt is the sheet, which becomes a cut.
- No more than one thing moves at a time, and it moves only because a hand caused it.
- No transition passes through a state that cannot be read: on paper the dim takes `--ink` 14.67 down to `--ink-soft` 8.73 and stops. On the night ground it does not take the verse and the name down into `--moon-faint`: they leave the screen, because 4.94 is 3.46 to her eye, and a line that fades into it has not dimmed, it has been erased slowly. The last line is never part of the ramp: it is set at the floor's ink on the ground it is on — `--ink-soft` 8.73 on paper, `--moon` 12.48 on kunda — and no frame of the dim is below 4.50 anywhere a letter is still meant to be read. `--moon-faint` stays the rule's ink and the tick's; every night letter that is not primary — a sentence, a name, a locator, a seal — is `--moon` 12.48 or `--moon-soft` 6.66, and neither faint ink is ever a sentence's, in the dim or out of it (A8, A12, I3, §2.6).
- No flash. A flash is three or more luminance steps inside half a second; the app has one ramp per ground change and none inside an act.

One tension is worth naming, because it is the kind of thing that is discovered in the field and not in the file. Reduced motion turns the 1200ms ground change into a cut, and a cut between `--paper` at 0.8808 and `--kunda` at 0.0163 is a full-screen luminance step — a flash by another name, at 04:20, in a dark room. So the platform's reduce-flash condition is read separately from its reduce-motion condition, and where it is set, the ground ramp keeps its 1200ms even under reduced motion (A7). A person who has asked for no movement has not asked to be flashed, and the two requests are not the same request.

## 8. Sound-off

**The app is complete with no sound at all.** Not usable-with-difficulty: complete. A person who is deaf, a person in a room with a sleeping child, a person whose speaker has filled with dust, and a person who has switched the phone to silent all get the same product, and none of them gets a lesser one.

| The sound | What it marks | The text that already says it | With sound off | What is lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The bell, 4s, armed per ārati | that this ārati has begun | the act's name and the line beneath it: "maṅgala-ārati has begun" | the same screen, the same words | the temperature of the moment, and nothing else |
| The bead, one wooden tick at 30% | that a bead was counted | the press itself; the count is never spoken | the press, and the haptic if the hand is not in public mode | a confirmation the finger already has |
| The haptic, 8ms | the same bead | the same | the press | the same |

The law is one sentence: **the bell never carries a meaning that the words do not also carry.** If a screen would not make sense with the sound removed, the sound was doing work that the words refused, and the words are wrong. That is why the bell is armed per ārati and not switched on for ever, why it never rings as a notification, and why the ārati screen says in words when the bell is not armed: a person who cannot hear it must not be left wondering whether it rang.

Two consequences follow and they are not small. The kīrtana surface cannot depend on hearing the kīrtana: `BRS 1.2.145` makes saṅkīrtana loud for the hearing of others, and the app can carry the words and a press to bring the next line, but it cannot be the sound — and it says so, in one line, rather than pretending. Nor does it listen: the call and the response are separated by a silence whose length is the recording's own, and the timing is carried by the hand that presses, because a microphone that waits for a man's voice in order to know when to sing again is the first ear, and this product has none (§3 of your review; `03` §6.2). And the japa surface with the screen reader on is silent by construction: the act is a press, the count is private, and the only thing that speaks is the sentence that ends the session.
