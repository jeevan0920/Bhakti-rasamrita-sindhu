## 0. What this document settles

Every string the product may print: in the eleven surfaces, on the lock screen, under a permission sheet, in an error, in the store listing, and in the one line that appears after a person has been away for a year. It also settles the strings that will be proposed to you in the next four months and must be refused at the door. It does not place a single screen; the frame decides how much of a string a person sees at once.

Three kinds of rule appear below, and each says which it is, so that you may dismiss the arithmetic and argue with the rest.

| Kind of rule | An example in this file | How you check it |
|---|---|---|
| A count | a sentence is at most fourteen words | count the words |
| A law, from your book | the app never praises a person | the locator beside it |
| A mechanical necessity | 32 characters to a line, so a sentence is three lines at most | the arithmetic in `01-FOUNDATIONS.md` §2.3 and §10.2 |

**One convention, and it is a quarantine.** The eleven words of your glossary appear in this document exactly once, in §2, because a list of things that may not be said has to name them. Outside §2 they do not occur, not even in a sentence describing the product, and the pattern the build runs against them lives inside §2.4 for the same reason. The other forbidden strings are quoted only where a refusal needs to name them: the bells of §5.4, the temptations of §9.

## 1. The register

### 1.1 Who is speaking

Two people are in the room and only one of them may talk. You are the first: you call a stranger *friend* and mean it, because you have taken prasāda with strangers for forty years and the word is a report of something you did. The app is the second. It has sat with nobody. It keeps the threshold swept and the lamp filled, and it does not speak while a person is praying.

So the app addresses one person, in the second person singular, in plain English, warm at the edges of an act and flat in the middle of one. It never says *we*, because there is no we: there is one person, one verse, and a clock. Where a sentence needs a speaker with authority it names the authority, not itself: *the text says*, *the reviewer is named at the foot of the page*, *the room meets at six*. It never uses a name, because it never asks for one, and a thing that has not been given a name may not use one.

### 1.2 The four distances, and the two the app may not come near

| Who is speaking | The line | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| You, at the gate hour | *My friend, you have come at a good hour.* | yours, and not transferable to a screen |
| The app, naming an act | The bell is at 04:20. | this is the register |
| The app, being clerical | The sādhaka is requested to attend the morning ārati. | refused: it addresses a form, and asks a man to think of himself as a category |
| The app, being chummy | Morning. Ready to rise? | refused: a question he did not ask for, from a thing that cannot sit with him |
| The app, being a shop | Begin your day with a little stillness. | refused: it sells the act by its feeling |

The last two are where a copy writer drifts on a Friday afternoon. The chummy one fails because intimacy is a claim about a relationship, and a false claim about a relationship is what *ābhāsa* names (BRS 4.9): the shape of a thing without its substance. The shop one fails because it attaches a benefit to an act, and your definition of *uttama-bhakti* is *anyābhilāṣitā-śūnyam*, devoid of any motive other than His pleasure (BRS 1.1.11). A sentence that offers calm in exchange for a practice has put a motive into the practice and called it a kindness.

### 1.3 What the app may never do to him

| It never | Because | The string it would have printed | What it prints |
|---|---|---|---|
| Praises | praise is a score with a compliment on it (R9) | Beautifully done. | nothing: the ring closes |
| Scolds | a missed act is a shape that is not there (R2) | You missed the morning. | nothing: the segment dims |
| Hurries | the hour is a time of day, never an interval (`01-FOUNDATIONS.md` §5.1) | Five minutes left. | the hour, 04:20 |
| Asks a question | a question demands an answer, and the app has no right to demand anything | How did that feel? | nothing |
| Promises | a promise about the future is a claim it cannot keep | We will remind you. | the act's name and its hour |
| Remembers for him | memory narrated back to a person is surveillance with a soft voice (R10) | Welcome back. You were away nine days. | The next ārati is at half past six. |
| Flatters progress | a percentage is a verdict on a person's inner state (R9) | You are almost through this chapter. | the verse's ordinal, a fact about the text |
| Apologises for itself | an apology makes the failure about the app's feelings | Sorry, something went wrong. | the three sentences of §4.6 |
| Claims a feeling | a feeling claimed by a thing is the counterfeit of a feeling | We have missed you. | nothing |

### 1.4 The three sentences of the register

1. **Name the act, or say nothing.** If a string cannot name something a person does with his hands, or a fact about the text, it has no work on the screen.
2. **A fact is enough.** A sentence that adds a feeling to a fact is a shop. *The bell is at 04:20* is a house; *the bell is at 04:20, do not miss it* is a shop.
3. **Read it aloud at 04:00, tired.** If the sentence would embarrass a tired man at four in the morning, it is not his sentence.

## 4. The sentence rules

### 4.1 The counts

| Unit | Maximum | The arithmetic behind the number |
|---|---|---|
| A sentence in a product string | 14 words, 84 characters | at 18/30 and 32 characters to the line, 84 characters is 2.6 lines: three lines, 90pt of the 522pt reading zone |
| A string on a screen | 2 sentences, 28 words | the zone holds 17.4 lines, 544 characters (`01-FOUNDATIONS.md` §10.2), and a screen that fills it has no room for a verse |
| An error | 3 sentences, 42 words | §4.6, and the third sentence is a pressable act |
| An act's label | 3 words | *Offer the lamp* is three; *japa* is one; a label that needs a comma is a sentence in the wrong place |
| A notification body | **54 characters** | a counted cap and not a geometry: the longest body in §5.3 is 52 characters, and 54 is that plus two characters of air. A bell is set in the system's own type size on a lock screen we do not own, so a line there is not a number this document can fix; what it can count is its own longest sentence. The ceiling was 60 in Round 1, and seven bodies ran past it, so the clause that got cut was the last one — and the last one is the only one that matters (objection 8) |
| A permission pre-line | 24 words | it must be read before the platform sheet covers it |
| The whole chrome of the first screen | 12 words | Seal VI: a stranger completes one act without instruction, and instruction is words |

### 4.2 Punctuation, all of it

| The mark | Verdict | The rule |
|---|---|---|
| Full stop | permitted | one at the end of a sentence, and no sentence without one; the provenance line and the short chain of §4.5 are citations and close without one |
| Comma | permitted | one per clause, never two in a row, never before *and* |
| Question mark | **never** | the app asks nothing; a question mark is a demand wearing a curve |
| Exclamation mark | **never** | in any string, any language, the store listing, a caption (charter §5) |
| Ellipsis | **never** | it withholds a word to make him wait, which is the mechanic this app refused (R5) |
| Dash, semicolon | **never in a string** | a dash is a breath, and the app is not the one breathing; two sentences are two sentences |
| Colon | permitted in one place only | inside a clock time: 04:20 |
| Middle dot | permitted in two places only | the provenance line at §4.5, as its separator: `BRS 1.1.11 · reviewer unnamed · translator unnamed`; and the third form's short chain at §4.5: `BRS 1.2.75 · not a translation`. It separates a citation's facts; it is never a sentence's pause, and no other string in the product carries it |
| Parentheses | **never** | a gloss is a comma and a phrase (§8.3), not a bracket |
| Quotation marks | never in the app's own voice | the verse may carry them; the chrome may not |
| Emoji, ampersand, slash, plus, asterisk | **never** | a mark standing in for a word is a word the writer did not choose, and a face is a feeling the app does not have |
| Capital letters | one exception, the locator (§4.5) | no all-caps for emphasis, no all-caps title, no all-caps word |

### 4.3 Case

Sentence case, everywhere, in every language the app speaks. Headings are sentence case. Notification titles are sentence case. A word is not capitalised to make it important. A common noun is never capitalised, and that includes every Sanskrit term: *japa*, *ārati*, *darśana*, *ekādaśī*, *dhāma*. A term capitalised becomes a proper noun, a proper noun becomes a brand, and a brand is the beginning of a shop.

The single exception is the locator, set in capitals because the small capitals the brief asked for are 14.4pt at an 18pt base and below your floor (`01-FOUNDATIONS.md` §12, D8). That exception is a citation, not a shout, and it licenses no other capital in the product.

### 4.4 Numerals

| The numeral | How it is written | Where | Never |
|---|---|---|---|
| The hour of the next act | figures, tabular, 24-hour, leading zero: 04:20 | the act zone | *in forty minutes*, *soon*, a countdown |
| The verse's ordinal in its chapter | figures, tabular: 11 | the foot of the margin | *verse 11 of 21*, a fraction, a percentage |
| The bead | figures, tabular, on request only | the japa screen, the one number he may switch off | any total of days |
| A provenance date | figures, tabular: 2024 | the chain, in the one line and at S-09 | a copyright line on the splash |

These four, and none of them counts him. In prose, a number that will fit in a word is a word: *one verse*, not *1 verse*; *three rounds*, not *3 rounds*; *the eleventh day*, not *the 11th day*. A numeral in a sentence is a figure pretending to be a fact, and most sentences that want one are counting something about him.

### 4.5 How a locator is written

| Written | Never written |
|---|---|
| BRS 1.1.11 | BRS 1:1:11, or BRS 1.1.11 (verse 11) |
| BRS 1.2.238 | BRS, first division, second chapter, verse 238 |
| Upadeśāmṛta 2 | Upadesamrta, verse two |

Division, chapter, verse: three groups of figures, full stops between, no spaces inside a group, one space between the work and the first figure. No page number, no edition year, no translator's name: those live in the chain. One short form of the work's name exists, **BRS** for *Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu*; *Upadeśāmṛta* is short enough to set in full; any other work is written out in full until you rule (open question 3).

The locator is a fact, not a control: never a link, never underlined, never coloured, and it does not change within a session. It tells him that a chain exists. **Where the chain is read changed in Round 2**, and it changed because the act on that screen changed: the reader's act is the hearing and not the inspection (objection 2), so the reader's provenance line at the foot of the reading surface carries the chain — the work, the verse, the reviewer, the date and the translator — and the full chain stands in the closing surfaces at S-09 Ācārya, which already exists for exactly this. The third form is the one exception, and it is not the reader's: it stands where a sentence of ours is read and no verse is printed, as it does at S-08.

**The line has three forms: two at the foot for a verse, and the third, a short form for a sentence that is ours.** Each is a string, because a pattern is not a string and the build prints strings. The two foot forms are the provenance line of a verse, and neither may be shortened or left with the reviewer out; the third form is not a shortened foot line and may never stand where a verse, a rendering or a gloss is printed. There is no fourth form.

**Unsigned, at the foot.** 50 characters.

> BRS 1.1.11 · reviewer unnamed · translator unnamed

**Signed, at the foot.** 55 characters.

> BRS 1.1.11 · reviewed by N. N., 14 March · trans. N. N.

Count them, because the count is the rule. The column at 18pt is 294 points and a character of Spectral is 9, so a line is 32.7 characters — 33, counted as a line — and the foot is allowed three lines: **99 characters**. The unsigned form is 50 and sits inside two of them; the signed form is 55 and also sits inside two. Neither is over 99, so neither can run to a fourth line. And neither carries a semicolon: the separator is the middle dot, which §4.2 permits in this line and in the third form below.

*N. N.* is where a name stands and *14 March* is where the day of the review stands; both are printed plainly, with no bracket and no blank, because a bracket here would be a form for someone to fill in rather than a citation. The line carries no full stop, because it is a citation and not a sentence in §4.2's sense: it ends when the facts end, and a full stop after *translator unnamed* would make it look like a claim that had finished being checked.

**No name is printed in it that the chain does not hold**, and no name is dropped from it to make it fit: the line prints the reviewer and the date and the translator's line, and a name too long for the 99 characters of three lines is a question for the chain at S-09 and for me, not a licence to print a fourth form. The line's work is to say that a person stood behind the words and when; the chain's work is to say who.

**Today nothing in the corpus is signed**, so the unsigned form is what the build prints everywhere, at the foot of the reading surface, in `--ink-soft` 8.73, horizontal at 18. The margin carries the locator, the ordinal and the seal, and it does not carry this word (§8.6). The signed form is what the line must print the morning after a person signs — which is your objection 9's law about the seal, carried into a line: it does not say a person stood behind the words until a person has.

**The third form, for a sentence that is ours.** 30 characters.

> BRS 1.2.75 · not a translation

The string is five words and the dot. It stands in the one line under a heading, where the sentence of the page is read, and it says the two things that line has to say before the first word: the limb's verse, and that the sentence is ours and not a translation. It is set at **18/24, horizontal**, in `--ink-soft` 8.73 by day and `--moon-soft` 6.66 at night. It keeps its 18 at 200% and at 300%, because at 36 on the 390 the sentence and the chain are five lines of 60, 300 points, and 4 + 56 + 300 = 360 in the 134 the zone holds; on the 320 they are six lines, 360 points, and the zone is no larger.

**30 is its ceiling, and the ceiling is a measure.** At 18 a character of Spectral is 9 points, and the narrowest column the line stands in is 272 at the 320, so 272 ÷ 9 = 30.2: the line holds 30 characters. 30 × 9 = 270, which leaves 2 points of air. The one line has three lines of that column — 90 characters — and the sentence above the chain takes 56 in two of them, which leaves the third to the chain: 56 + 30 = 86 in 90. The form he weighed instead, `BRS 1.2.75 · the sentence is not a translation`, is 46 characters, and 46 + 56 = 102, which is 12 past the 90 and a fourth line; 4 + 56 + 96 = 156 in the 134, 22 over. The 30 is not a shorter way of saying what the 46 said; it is the longest form that keeps the page's count, and the sentence it cannot carry is spoken whole at S-08's reader script.

**What the third form may never replace.** It never replaces the foot's two forms: the foot line is for a verse, and the third form prints no verse, no rendering and no gloss. It never carries a reviewer or a translator cell, because it renders nothing those cells could hold — and those cells are not dropped from the foot's line to make room for it. It never replaces the full chain at S-09. It is not a fourth form and there is no fourth. A page that prints a verse uses the foot's two; a page that prints a sentence of ours uses the third form; no page uses both.

### 4.6 How an error is written

Three sentences, in this order, and the third names a pressable act: **what happened**, in the past tense, with no code and no apology; **what is still true**, because something always is, and it is the sentence that decides whether he is afraid; **what to do next**, as an act and not as advice.

| The failure | The three sentences |
|---|---|
| A voice note at 04:00, no network | The recording did not reach the server. It is still on this phone, whole. It will go when the phone finds a network. |
| A festival page on a loaded day | The festival page did not open. The verses for tonight are on the phone. Open the reader, and the page will be here tomorrow. |
| A journal page that did not save | The page did not save. What you wrote is still on this screen. Press the mark once more. |
| A recitation that did not download | The recitation did not download. The verses are on the phone without it. Press Hear it again, and it will try the network once more. |
| A reply that did not leave | The reply did not leave the phone. It is saved in the room. It will go when the network returns. |

What is never in an error: a code, alone or at all; a colour; an icon of alarm; a question mark; the word *failed* (§2.2); an apology; a suggestion to contact support or check the connection; an asterisk; a frowning face. An error is the least coloured thing in the app, and that is deliberate, because nothing here is an alarm (`00-DESIGN-BRIEF.md` §2, H1). An error also never interrupts an act: it waits on the page that owns it, and if he opened the app for maṅgala at 04:20, last night's failed recording is not mentioned until he returns to the journal.

**The festival's name is not in the failure.** The failure's block is 342 wide at the 390 and 272 at the 320, and *Janmāṣṭamī* at 54 is 308: an *m* at that size is nearer 46 than 27, so the word is past 272 before its tenth character. At 36 the name is 205 in the 224 that S-11's [C] gives it, and that is where the page sets the name when it prints it at all (S-11 §5 [C], objection 4). A sentence cannot be capped, so the failure names the page and not the name, and the name stays whole on the screen that carries the day.

### 4.7 The three tests every string must pass

1. **The aloud test.** Read it at 04:00 as a tired man would read it. If it embarrasses, rewrite.
2. **The shop test.** Could the sentence stand on a shop's window without changing a word. If yes, it is selling something.
3. **The deletion test.** Delete the string. Does the screen still work. If it does, the string was decoration, and decoration in a temple competes with the deity for attention (charter §4).

### 5.2 The hours

Three a day at most, at ārati junctions he has armed, never between 21:00 and 04:00, never on the night of ekādaśī (spec §9).

## 6. The permission asks

### 6.1 The five laws of asking

1. **Never on first open.** The threshold has no sheet and no explanation of what the app will need. A person who has been in the building for nine seconds is not asked for the keys.
2. **Ask at the act it serves**, after the act has been done once without it. The journal asks for the microphone at the first press and hold of *Speak*, and never before.
3. **The screen behind the sheet has already said, in our words, what is about to be asked and why** (`01-FOUNDATIONS.md` §1.3). The platform writes its own sheet and we cannot recolour it; we can make sure the sentence under it is ours.
4. **A refusal is not a wall.** Every ask has a refusal state in which the act still works, less one faculty, and the app says what is still true.
5. **Never asked twice in the same act**, never re-asked on a schedule, and one reminder only, in the closing surfaces, after the act has been used three times without the faculty.

### 6.2 The asks

| The ask | What it serves | When it is asked | The line before the sheet | If he refuses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Notifications | the āratis he arms | the end of the first ārati he completes, never at first open | The clock would ring three times a day, at the āratis you arm. Never between nine at night and four in the morning. | The ring keeps the day without the bell. The act zone shows the next act and the hour. |
| Microphone | a voice note in the journal, and nothing else | at the first press and hold of *Speak*, and never before | Your voice is recorded only while you are speaking, and kept for the note alone. The note stays on this phone. | The keyboard is offered in its place, and the journal is whole without a voice. Nothing else in the app is changed, because nothing else in the app uses a microphone. |
| Camera | nothing | never | *(no sheet, because there is no ask)* | nothing is lost, because nothing was asked for |
| Storage | the downloaded divisions and one map | when he asks to download a division, on a network he chose | The corpus's text is under seven megabytes. One division's recitation is about forty. Both stay on the phone and can be removed here. | Nothing already downloaded is lost. The reader works with what is on the phone, and says nothing about what is not. |
| Location | a room near him; a map that knows where he is standing | when he joins a room, or opens a dhāma map | The room is chosen by where you are, once. The app keeps no history of where you have been. | He types a locality. The map works from the downloaded file, and the app does not ask again. |

**The ask is raised by the act's own verb.** The first press and hold of *Speak* stands before the microphone opens, once, and the sound begins on the next press; there is no mark to press and no target outside the act starts a note (S-07 §5 [E], §10.12). A page whose ask was refused is a page with a field and no note: its act's word is *Keep*, and it waits for words, which is the refusal path below and not a second screen.

**The microphone, and the refusal.** The ask above is smaller than it was in Round 1, and the sentence you refused is struck. *The microphone hears the response so the call can wait for it* was written to make a kīrtana easier, and it was wrong for the reason you gave: anubhāva is on the side of the one who loves, and a machine that waits for a man's voice in order to know when to sing again has begun to listen to him (`BRS 2.1.3–5`, and the corpus's own warning in `01` §7 about the crack through which a sensor for tears would later be pushed). So response mode does not listen. It works like this, and this is the whole of its design:

1. the call plays, and the recording's own silence follows it — the length of the silence is the recording's, not the app's;
2. a press brings the next line;
3. the timing is carried by the hand, which is the only instrument this product has ever allowed itself.

The band's word in response mode is *Next line*, because that is what a press does, and a word that named a rhythm the app cannot hear would be the promise the microphone was refused for. If the person wants the tempo of a kīrtana, kīrtana is at S-03 with the words, the call and the response written out, and it asks nothing of his voice either. A note he makes in the journal is still his own voice, kept for himself, which is the one thing you allowed; that is why the ask above survives at all, and why its sentence is about a note and not about a room.

**The camera.** There is no camera permission in this build, and the reason is not squeamishness. Nothing here needs to see: the eight involuntary symptoms are never read from a body (spec §8, R10), a photograph of a person's practice is a share card one step removed (Seal III), and the product's two photographs were taken by us — of a form and of a lamp, with their provenance set in the margin (`01` §1.3; `04` §2.5). If a later build wants a camera for a place, that is a different argument and it needs your seal in writing, because a photograph of a place is not a reading of a person and the difference must be written down before the first exception, not after.

**Storage and the others.** No broad file access is requested on either platform; downloads live in the app's own directory and are removed by one act in the closing surfaces. The app never asks for the photo library, the contacts, the calendar, or the health store.

## 7. The hard moments

Nine moments, and the strings for each. This is the section a team writes worst, because each is a place where the app is tempted to feel something, and a thing that feels something at a person is a thing that is performing.

### 7.1 A missed day

The clock shows an unmarked segment, which is to say it shows a gap. No sentence on the clock, no badge, no banner. One line is permitted the next morning, at the foot of the reading zone, and it may be switched off in the closing surfaces (spec §7, F1).

> Nothing was marked yesterday. Nothing is owed. The next ārati is at noon.

Not said: *You missed*, *Do not worry*, *Try again today*, the number of days, the words *fresh* and *reset*.

### 7.2 A missed week

The same gap, six times, and no sentence for six days. On the seventh day one line appears once, and does not appear again for that absence.

> The ring has unmarked days in it. It keeps no account of them. The next act is at half past six.

Not said: a welcome, an offer to begin again, a claim that his place was kept, an ember, a candle, a small returning animation.

### 7.3 A broken practice of eleven months

He comes back on a Tuesday. The app may not know the number, and the number may not appear. What it can say is the one thing that is true and that a debt would have made false.

> The lamp is where it was. Nothing was lost while you were away. The next ārati is at half past six.

Not said: *Welcome back*, *It has been a while*, *We have kept your place*, *You can start again*. The last is refused because *start again* narrates his practice for him, and the app does not know what happened in eleven months. It knows that a phone was not opened. The ring shows the marks it has; it does not shade the gap, and it does not offer to fill it.

### 7.4 A death in the room

The host marks it, and the mark is a fact entered by a person. The app's part is two things: the death and the closure. There is no act.

> Kamalā has died.

> The room is closed.

The death stands in the head, where the host's name stood; *The room is closed.* is the thread's first line, and the names that were in it stand under it. The two are also the spoken stop 2, in that order. The hour is not spoken at any stop: the room's own hour is not kept in the bereaved state, and no stop says when the room sits or that it is waiting. The band holds the door alone, and the door says *close*. The room's bells are off while the room is closed, and the app does not say so, because an announcement of the app's own tact is a performance of it.

Not said: *Our condolences*, *In loving memory*, *Gone to a better place*, *Share a memory*, a black band, a ribbon, a lotus, a lit candle drawn in `--lamp`, which would break the second law of colour for the worst possible reason.

**And the same room from the other side.** If the person holding the phone is the one who is dying, the app is one screen and one act: the verse he last read, and in the act band the name alone, which needs no eyes. Seal VIII and Seal IX are why the act zone is a band at the thumb and never a list.

> The verse is where you left it.

### 7.5 A reported post

Two people, two strings, and the reporter is told less than he would like.

| To | The string | When |
|---|---|---|
| The reporter, immediately | This has gone to a person who will read it. Nothing further is asked of you. | on the press, once |
| The writer, if a person removes the note | A person read this note and removed it. The reason is one press away. | when the decision is made, by a person |
| The writer, if a person leaves it standing | *(nothing)* | never |
| Anyone, ever | This violates our community guidelines. | never: a policy is not a person, and a rule quoted at a person is a wall (charter §15, vow 2) |

The reporter is never told the outcome, because an outcome told to a reporter makes reporting a lever. The writer is never told who reported, and is told nothing at all unless a named person has acted.

### 7.6 A deleted journal

Deletion is the one act that cannot be undone, so it is the one screen that carries `--copper` and nothing else coloured (`01-FOUNDATIONS.md` §1.3). H1's sentence is exact about where the copper may stand — *no letter is ever set in it* — so the copper is a 1pt rule above the act and the whole sentence below it is `--ink`, because a sentence a frightened person must read is not a place for a colour that is doing decorative work. No letter on this screen is copper, and no letter anywhere in this document is: the copper is a rule and a mark, and the sentence that says *cannot* is ink on paper. The confirmation is not a question, because the app asks nothing.

> This deletes the page. There is no copy on the server, and none in this app. It cannot be undone.

After it, in the same place:

> The page is deleted. The verse it came from is still here.

Not said: *Are you sure?*, *This action is permanent*, a warning triangle, a red band, *We are sad to see it go*.

### 7.7 A failed upload at 04:00

He spoke into the journal at four in the morning and the note did not leave the phone.

> The recording did not reach the server. It is still on this phone, whole. It will go when the phone finds a network.

The line waits on the journal page and is not shown at 04:20 over maṅgala. The act band still says *maṅgala-ārati*, because the app is not going to make him choose between a failure and an ārati at four in the morning.

Not said: *Upload failed*, a code, a red mark, *Check your connection*, a retry spinner, *We will try again in five minutes*.

### 7.8 A festival that will not load

The page is heavy, the network is thin, and this is the one day of the year it must not fail.

> The festival page did not open. The verses for tonight are on the phone. Open the reader, and the page will be here tomorrow.

The third sentence names an act, and the act works, because the reader was downloaded weeks ago. The app does not apologise for the network, blame it, or pretend the page is coming in a moment.

Not said: *High traffic*, *Please try later*, *Something went wrong*, a shimmer, a skeleton, a clock face that turns while nothing happens.

### 7.9 A person crying and typing

The detection is on the device, and it is a check for language of self-harm, not a reading of a person (spec §12). It fires once. The line appears under what he wrote, in ink, and it does not ask a question.

> What you wrote is heavier than this page can hold. There are people who sit with this, and one of them is awake now.

Under it, the local line, verified quarterly by a named person against the country he is in. Under that stands the screen's sealed label, one sentence, because it is the sentence that lets him keep writing:

> Nothing you wrote has left this phone.

The label is `S07.label.sealed`, 38 characters and 7 words, and the crisis block's last sentence is the same string, `S07.crisis.kept`: the head's standing claim and the block's moment are one sentence in two places, and this document prints it once. It says where the words are and makes no claim about a reader — the check reads the language, and a sentence that says the page is unread would be false of the device that has just read it. The old claim about who had not read the page is struck, and no second sentence stands beside this one.

And then the app does nothing. No second line, no follow-up bell, no check the next morning, no change to any other screen. The next notification is the ordinary bell at the ordinary hour, and it does not refer to this. That silence is the hardest string in the document, and it is a string: it was chosen, and it is reviewed.

Not said: *Are you okay?*, *We are here for you*, *Help is available*, *Your feelings matter*, *Talk to someone*, a heart, a hand, a circle of dots, a pause before the line appears so that it seems to have been considered.

## 8. Sanskrit and Devanagari

### 8.1 The principle

The app may print the Sanskrit for **what he does with his hands**. It may not print the Sanskrit for **what is happening inside him**. The first is a name for an act, shorter and truer than the English. The second is a classification of his state, and a person handed a taxonomy of his own heart will begin to grade himself against it (R9).

| Printed, unglossed after first use | Never printed |
|---|---|
| japa, ārati and the eight junction names, darśana, praṇāma, prasāda, ekādaśī, dhāma, sevā, tulasī, kuṇḍa, saṅga, smaraṇa, śravaṇa, nāma, pradakṣiṇā, ācārya | vibhāva, sthāyī-bhāva, vyabhicārī-bhāva, sāttvika-bhāva, adhikāra, ābhāsa, rasa, aṅga, sādhaka, anartha, śraddhā, ruci, āsakti |

The right-hand column is the ledger's language, the taxonomy's, and ours in this file. It is exact, and it is not the app's. A person told he is in *anartha-nivṛtti* has been given a stage, and your spec §3 forbids a stage from being shown for the plainest reason: it becomes a place to be, and then a place to be above.

### 8.2 When a term is declined, and how

| Rule | The form | Never |
|---|---|---|
| Used as a common noun in an English sentence, it takes English grammar | two āratis, the hour of the ārati | āratīḥ, āratīnām |
| Quoted as a term, it takes no ending | the word pradakṣiṇā | the word pradakṣiṇām |
| No possessive apostrophe on a term | the hour of the ārati | the ārati's hour |
| Diacritics in every case, including a title | maṅgala-ārati | mangala-arati, Mangala Arati |
| Lowercase in every position, including the head of a sentence | — | Ārati is at six |
| A hyphen inside a compound, split nowhere except at the hyphen | anyābhilāṣitā-śūnyam | anyābhilāṣitāśūnyam |

A sentence never begins with a Sanskrit term. If it would, the sentence is rewritten, because a term at the head of a sentence takes a capital, and a capital makes a practice into a person.

### 8.3 The gloss

Glossed at the first use on a screen, in the same line, after a comma, in `--ink-soft`, and never again on that screen. The gloss is not in the margin: the margin holds the locator, the seal, the ordinal, and one heading (`01-FOUNDATIONS.md` §3.2), and a gloss there would be a fourth thing and a species of caption.

| The term | The gloss as it prints | Characters |
|---|---|---|
| japa | the name, repeated | 18 |
| ārati | the lamp offered at the hour | 28 |
| darśana | the seeing of the form | 22 |
| praṇāma | a bow, sent | 11 |
| ekādaśī | the eleventh day, a fast | 24 |
| dhāma | a place where He walked | 23 |
| pradakṣiṇā | three rounds, and then it ends | 30 |
| smaraṇa | remembering, written down | 25 |

Four rules for a gloss. It is a description, not a translation of a verse. It never uses a second Sanskrit term to explain the first. It is reviewed like any other sentence, because it is one. And the glossary itself stands with the chain, in the closing surfaces at S-09, because text is not tappable (`01` §4.2) and because the reader's act is now the hearing: a glossary pulled out of a verse would be the second act on a screen that has one (H5, objection 2). A gloss is printed at first use on the screen that needs it, and the glossary is a room a person walks to, not a drawer he pulls out of a verse.

### 8.4 No Sanskrit to make a plain thing holy

| The plain thing | The temptation | Why it is refused | What is printed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Delete | tyāga | renunciation is a practice, and a button named after a practice makes the practice a button | Delete this page |
| Settings | ācārya | the ācārya is a person who can be asked a question, and a list cannot | Notifications, sound, and the day |
| Search | jijñāsā | inquiry is a relation to a teacher (aṅga 5), not a field | Search the corpus |
| A counter | sādhanā-saṅkalpa | a vow is not a counter, and giving a counter a vow's name is the counterfeit Gate IV exists for | no word at all: the ring |
| An error | aparādha | an offence is a doctrinal category with a remedy; a failed upload is not one, and naming it so is a lie about his standing | the three sentences of §4.6 |

The test is mechanical and it is the only one needed: **if the plain English word would do, and the Sanskrit is there because the plain word sounded ordinary, remove the Sanskrit.** A term stays when the English would be a paraphrase and a worse one, and when a person who practises already uses the term for the thing he does.

### 8.5 Devanagari

| Where it may appear | Where it may never appear |
|---|---|
| the verse, in the reading zone | a button, a label, a tab |
| the work's title on the provenance sheet | an error, a permission line, a notification |
| the verse's incipit, where a chain cites it | a heading, a caption, a watermark, an icon, a splash |
| | a single akṣara used as ornament, a background texture, a pattern |

Devanagari is never letterspaced, faux-bolded, italic, underlined, rotated, or set in a weight the bundle does not carry (`01-FOUNDATIONS.md` §2.3), and never used as decoration, because a sacred thing used as decoration is the first refusal in your brief (H9). The wordmark is Latin and stays Latin: `laharī`, lowercase, always. And no compound and no word of his is ever broken at the column: where कृष्णानुशीलनं will not fit the measure at the size a person has chosen, the block moves and the eye walks (I6), because a space is where a line may break and a joint inside a word is not.

A notification body may carry a Sanskrit term only if every glyph in it is present in the platform's own default family at system size. The six that pass are *maṅgala-ārati*, *japa*, *rāja-ārati*, *sandhyā-ārati*, *śrīmad-bhāgavatam*, and *ekādaśī*, and they are the six in §5.3. A term that has not been checked is not sent to a lock screen, where a missing glyph renders as a box, and a box where a word should be is the app failing in public.

### 8.6 The unreviewed rule

A verse, a translation, a gloss, or a term whose reviewer is not named carries the absence in words, in exactly one place: the provenance line at the foot of the reading surface, in `--ink-soft` 8.73, set horizontally at 18, in the unsigned form of §4.5 — *BRS 1.1.11 · reviewer unnamed · translator unnamed*. The screen prints *reviewer unnamed*; *unreviewed* is this document's name for that word. The word is never in the margin, never vertical, and never in `--ink-faint`: the margin carries locators, of which the verse's ordinal is the last figure, and reviewer seals (§4.5).
